Will the Stock Market Crash in 2026? 
Geopolitical conflict and economic instability fuel investor anxiety despite underlying business growth and resilience.
The S&P 500 is treading water in 2026, with the index up only 0.35% as of March 4, leaving analysts wondering if the stock market can absorb another pressure point.
That vibe intensified in the past week after the U.S. and Israel bombed around 1,000 targets in Iran, taking out the country's political leadership, including Ali Khamenei, the supreme leader. U.S. President Donald Trump said the attacks were meant to thwart Iran's rising nuclear arsenal threat and usher in a new era of political freedom in the country.

While the stock market has held firm since the attacks began on Feb. 28, investors are assessing the ongoing conflict in the Middle East. The U.S.-Iran war, coupled with tariff turbulence, rising U.S. job cuts and shaky consumer confidence, adds to market risks right now, leaving investors anxious about how much pressure the stock market can handle.
"The economy entered 2026 at a time of transition from consumer spending to business investment," says David Russell, global head of market strategy at TradeStation Group. "This created a strange environment where earnings grew despite many Americans feeling unhappy about the economy. Stocks were on a stronger footing than many perceived because growth was in areas like data center construction and capital investment."
That scenario has resulted in an off-balance stock market that may leave investors wondering what happens next, especially given the [military conflict in Iran](https://money.usnews.com/investing/articles/strait-of-hormuz-gridlock-how-the-u-s-iran-conflict-impacts-oil-prices-and-more). Is it enough to topple the stock market in 2026?
Economic and investment experts point to a slew of market risks to weigh right now, with these issues topping the list:

  * [Mixed messages from the Federal Reserve.](https://money.usnews.com/investing/articles/will-the-stock-market-crash-risk-factors#mixed-messages)
  * [Tech sector concentration.](https://money.usnews.com/investing/articles/will-the-stock-market-crash-risk-factors#tech-sector)
  * [Mounting energy risks.](https://money.usnews.com/investing/articles/will-the-stock-market-crash-risk-factors#energy-risks)
  * [Job market jitters.](https://money.usnews.com/investing/articles/will-the-stock-market-crash-risk-factors#job-market)
  * [Evolution of artificial intelligence.](https://money.usnews.com/investing/articles/will-the-stock-market-crash-risk-factors#ai-evolution)


## The Iran Conflict
In this case, market impact will depend largely on the duration and scope of the U.S.-Iran military conflict. "Our base case is that it lasts weeks rather than months," says Crit Thomas, global market strategist at Touchstone Investments. "President Trump has repeatedly expressed a desire for [lower interest rates](https://money.usnews.com/investing/articles/interest-rate-cut-stocks), lower oil prices and a weaker dollar."
Political leadership will influence the duration of the war, but markets will ultimately respond to those economic effects. "In other words, if our base case is wrong and the war extends for months, [stock market risks](https://money.usnews.com/investing/articles/high-return-low-risk-investments-for-retirees) are likely to intensify," Thomas adds.
The next big risks are how political leaders react to the crisis. Do they seek de-escalation, or prolong the fighting? Will central bankers bend to political pressure and keep interest rates too low? "Credit markets could be another risk, and there have been concerns about private credit and heavy borrowing by hyperscalers," Russell says. "Those could become problematic if interest rates increase."
## Mixed Messages From the Federal Reserve
Another big market risk is the 3% annual rise in core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) for December 2025. "This indicates that the Federal Open Market Committee may have overheated the U.S. economy by not applying monetary policy restrictively enough for five consecutive years," says John Blank, chief equity strategist at Zacks Investment Research.
Economic discussions at the top are concerning to some investors, especially with a new Fed chair poised to take over. "Fed monetary policy may be in a dependent state of governance, where populist politics complicate the stabilization of prices while maintaining full employment," Blank adds. "Greater extremes in outcomes are now possible for the U.S. economy in the second half of 2026 and beyond."
The Fed's next moves will trigger major scrutiny in the markets, other experts say.
"I expect four cuts to the Fed's overnight interest rate in 2026 after Kevin Warsh takes over for (current Fed Chair) Jerome Powell in May," says Brian Huckstep, chief investment officer at Advyzon Investment Management. "Our economy doesn't need these rate cuts, but I expect that President Trump's mandate to cut rates will be dutifully carried out by his new appointee."
Plus, the recent military actions in Iran are adding new rationale for rate cuts. "The Fed's overnight rate factors directly into stock valuations through the discount rate, and I expect rate cuts to be a tailwind for stock prices in 2026," Huckstep says.
## Tech Sector Concentration
The U.S. economy enters 2026 in reasonably healthy shape by traditional macroeconomic indicators. Gross domestic product, or GDP, growth projections range from roughly 2.2% (Congressional Budget Office) to 2.8%, Goldman Sachs reports. "Growth continues to be supported by consumer spending, [AI-driven capital expenditure](https://money.usnews.com/investing/articles/artificial-intelligence-stocks-the-10-best-ai-companies) and fiscal incentives such as targeted tax credits," says Angie Malltezi, chief operating officer at Altius, a digital infrastructure company.
That said, the composition of growth matters. "A significant portion of current economic momentum is concentrated in large-scale investment cycles, particularly artificial intelligence infrastructure, semiconductors and power generation," Malltezi says.
The upside is clear, Malltezi adds: "An [investment boom in computing](https://money.usnews.com/investing/articles/best-quantum-computing-stocks-to-buy), chips and energy capacity." The risk, however, is concentration. "When equity leadership and capital expenditure are heavily clustered in a narrow set of themes, sentiment reversals tend to be sharper and more violent," Malltezi says.

## Mounting Energy Risks
Steven Rogé, chief investment officer and CEO of R.W. Rogé & Co., says that so far in 2026, his team sees a healthy economy with steady growth and durable earnings, which support continued expansion. "The market carries added risk because valuations are elevated, and expectations are demanding," Rogé says. "If earnings continue to expand and inflation remains contained, markets can continue to hold steady."
Yet geopolitics sits at the center of the risk map, especially developments involving Iran and the broader Middle East, which supplies energy to China, India and much of Europe. "Disruptions to oil production or shipping routes can quickly raise inflation and increase costs for businesses," Rogé says. "[Higher energy prices](https://money.usnews.com/investing/funds/articles/energy-etfs-to-buy-now) function like a tax on consumers and a margin squeeze on businesses. Markets can digest uncertainty when supply chains remain intact, repricing quickly when energy faces sustained pressure."
The central energy market variable is the region's Strait of Hormuz. Roughly one-fifth of globally traded oil and about 20% of global liquefied natural gas, or LNG, traverse this geographical chokepoint daily. "Bypass pipeline capacity covers only a fraction of Gulf exports, leaving millions of barrels per day without viable alternative routes in a disruption scenario," says Malltezi. "Because major Asian economies account for the majority of these flows, any interruption would be global in consequence."
Crucially, the strait need not be fully closed to cause economic damage. "Even partial disruption triggers immediate risk aversion among shippers and insurers, constraining flow and pushing prices higher," Malltezi says.
Once supply is impaired, downstream effects should move quickly. "We could see refineries delay runs awaiting shipments, manufacturing plants throttle production, and energy scarcity begin feeding into everything that moves or gets made," Malltezi adds.
## Labor Market Jitters
Another major risk factor economists are watching is labor market deterioration driven by cyclical weakness in demand. "Initial unemployment claims have historically provided a timely signal for corporate earnings trends," Thomas says. "Companies typically turn to job cuts as a last resort, so rising jobless claims can signal emerging earnings stress."
The market did see an increase in layoffs in 2025, but those were concentrated in government-related job cuts rather than private-sector demand weakness. "At present, claims data does not suggest any broad-based economic stress," Thomas says. "Secondarily, we are watching [inflation](https://money.usnews.com/investing/stock-market-news/articles/stocks-that-are-good-inflation-investments), as it is important in determining the next move by the Fed."
## Evolution of AI
It's not easy being an investor with a long-term horizon of five years or more, given the overwhelming impact of artificial intelligence on the economy and markets.
"Over that time frame, we believe the largest opportunity and risk is the evolution of AI," Thomas says. "How AI changes our economy, businesses it will disrupt, and new industries it will create, are all unknowns right now."
With AI casting a big shadow, Thomas recommends taking a [well-diversified approach to the stock market](https://money.usnews.com/investing/articles/international-dividend-stocks-for-diversification), but otherwise staying the course. "Historically, being out of the market has posed a greater risk to long-term wealth accumulation than staying invested through a period of volatility," he says.
## **Where to Put $25,000 to $50,000 Right Now**
Investors with some extra cash on the sidelines should put it to work in the stock market, but carefully. "If you have, say, $25,000 or $50,000 to invest today, start by assigning every dollar a job," Rogé says.
Short-term liabilities and near-term spending belong in bucket one. "That move emphasizes stability and liquidity in savings accounts, money markets or [short-term investment grade bonds](https://money.usnews.com/investing/articles/best-short-term-investments-for-income)," he notes. "Intermediate needs sit in bucket two, which should be filled with intermediate investment-grade bonds, Treasury bonds and catastrophe bonds for added diversification."
Long-term capital that aims to outpace inflation over 10-plus years resides in bucket three, with diversified equity, gold bullion and liquid alternatives such as managed futures. "Once the purpose is clear, the allocation becomes straightforward, and markets tend to reward patience when capital is matched to the right timeline," Rogé adds.
