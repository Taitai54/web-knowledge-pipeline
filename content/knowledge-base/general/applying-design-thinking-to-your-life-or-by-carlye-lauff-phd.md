# Applying Design Thinking to Your Life

> “Above all, think of life as a prototype. We can conduct experiments, make discoveries, and change our perspectives…” _–Tim Brown, CEO of IDEO_

When I came across this quote, it dawned on me — each and every one of our lives are complex, messy problems ready to be solved. Why not use design thinking principles to help us figure out our passions and our careers? I’d rather rapidly prototype, test, and iterate outcomes for my life now instead of wishing I had tried to be a yoga instructor, book author, or toy designer in fifty years.

If design thinking can transform organizations and inspire innovation, then surely it can help us mere humans create a life that is both meaningful and fulfilling. And this philosophy and approach is for everyone — regardless of age, occupation, or current situation — design thinking can help you to uncover what you should try next. And the best part is that if you fail or absolutely hate the experience, then you failed now and _failed forward_— you have one less option to worry about and you’ve moved towards uncovering what will make you happy long-term.

I am at a unique cross-road in my life right now, which has forced me to come back to this “designing your life” mindset. I am about to graduate with my PhD in Design Theory and Methodology, and I have to decide what to do next…academic? design consultant? circus performer? barista? So many of us have moments like this, whether we are about to graduate or are just unhappy or unmotivated in our current careers. If you are thinking to yourself right now, “I wonder what it would be like to be an artist” or “My childhood dream was to be a musician” or “I hate my job but I’m afraid I wasted my college degree”, then **I challenge you to join me in this journey of applying design thinking to our lives in the pursuit of creating a joyful and well-lived life.\***

Design thinking is an innovation management philosophy that has five core tenants to solving complex problem: 1) empathize, 2) define, 3) ideate, 4) prototype, and 5) test.

1. **Empathy:** The first step to designing your life is by taking a critical look at yourself. You need to understand where you are right now — professionally, mentally, physically, relationship-wise — to then see where you’re going next. You need to ask yourself some hard questions, like when was the last time you felt truly happy? What makes you smile and laugh? What do you wish you’d never have to do again? You need to dig deep into yourself and assess your experiences, jobs, relationships, and extracurricular activities up until this point. Beware of dysfunctional thinking — do not get stuck in the idiosyncrasies of age old career advice like _“what is your passion?”_. Often you can’t be passionate about something until you’ve had a lifetime of experience!
2. **Define:** The second stage is about defining what it is that you desire as an outcome of your life. It is only through critically understanding yourself — what you do, who you are, and what you believe — that you can begin to connect the dots and see a path for where you’re going. It is often helpful to make a list of things you desire to be a part of your life; think of these as the ingredients for success. Part of this stage is re-framing, one of my favorite tools as a designer. You flip conventional thinking and assumptions on their head based on the empathy work you’ve done. And don’t get stuck trying to solve “gravity problems” — the things you cannot change! Instead of blaming gravity for making it hard to bike up a hill, maybe re-frame this challenge as how to make a lighter bicycle or maybe forget bikes altogether and think about other modes of transportation like a personal jet-packs (totally possible in our lifetime).
3. **Ideate:** Here comes the fun part! You get to imagine different five and ten-year scenarios for your life. Start with three options—from one that may be pretty traditional to your background to one that is something you’d do if money wasn’t a factor and if you didn’t care what other people thought _(that’s a whole other issue not for this post)_. Be careful about having too many choices though, as research has proven that too many options can create _choice overload_ where you result in significantly less happiness due to the “but what if…” or FOMO (fear of missing out) mindsets.
4. **Prototype:** Now, you get to prototype these future lives. What does a prototype of your life look like? Well, they can be conversations and interviews, shadowing of positions, or short or long term experiences. If you want to be a circus performer, then you better talk to a few performers to learn the ins-and-outs of the job and industry. It might not be as glamorous as you thought, or maybe you’ll learn that there are aspects that are better than you imagined. Conversations are easy and free, so have as many as you can! To take your life prototypes another step further, you could shadow positions or sign up for a short-term course. If you want to be a pastry chef, then you should try a dessert cooking class to see how much you enjoy cooking in real life versus watching the Food Network and drooling at home.
5. **Test (and iterate):** Remember that each of these prototypes — whether conversations or experiences —need to happen _in the real world!_ You can’t just sit here and think about what those jobs may be like. You need to see first-hand. If you don’t like these experiences, then head back to stage one and start all over again. Design, and life, are not linear processes. It is rare that you’ll find the perfect job after the first interview. Instead, take the time to reflect on your experiences and document what you liked and did not like. This might help to re-frame some goals for your life moving forward.

**Life is anything but linear and there is no perfect outcome. There are many versions of the best life for each of us. It is all about perspective and being confident that you’ve tried out the handful of different options that might have been next-best. Design thinking is a helpful tool that anyone can use to evaluate their life and happiness. If you’re not happy, then do something about it. Have curiosity and a bias towards action, and I promise you won’t be disappointed with how your life turns out.**

> As Bill Burnett says, “It’s simple…get curious, talk to people, and try stuff.”

If you are interested in learning more about designing your life, then I highly recommend reading _“_ [_Designing Your Life: How to Build a Well-Lived, Joyful Life_](http://designingyour.life/) _”_ by Bill Burnett and Dave Evans and watching this 25-minute TEDxStanford talk.

Bill Burnett’s 2017 TEDxStanford talk on Designing Your Life
