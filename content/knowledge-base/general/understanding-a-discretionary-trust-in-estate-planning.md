## What Is a Discretionary Trust?
A discretionary trust is a legal arrangement where the person creating the trust, known as the grantor or settlor, gives a trustee the authority to decide when and how much to distribute to each beneficiary. Instead of setting fixed amounts or dates for distribution, the trustee uses discretion to act in the best interests of the beneficiaries based on evolving needs and circumstances.
This flexible model is often used when the grantor wants to provide long-term protection and adaptability, especially when beneficiaries are minors, financially inexperienced, or in unstable personal situations. It’s a tool that aligns closely with the realities of modern families, blended households, and multigenerational wealth planning.
## How It Differs from a Fixed Trust
In a fixed trust, the terms are rigid: beneficiaries receive set amounts or percentages at specific times. With a discretionary trust, the trustee holds the power to choose how trust income and principal are allocated. There may be guidelines, but not requirements. This structure is especially helpful when the future needs of beneficiaries are uncertain.
For example, if one child develops a medical condition or financial hardship, the trustee can choose to provide additional support, without needing to change the trust terms. This adaptability helps families respond to life as it unfolds.
## Benefits of a Discretionary Trust
  * **Asset Protection:** Assets in a discretionary trust are generally shielded from lawsuits, divorce settlements, and creditor claims.
  * **Tax Flexibility:** Trustees can time distributions in ways that reduce tax exposure for both the trust and beneficiaries.
  * **Preserving Eligibility:** Beneficiaries receiving government assistance may not lose benefits if distributions are made strategically.
  * **Avoiding Family Conflict:** Since distributions are not guaranteed, there’s less entitlement—and potentially less resentment—among beneficiaries.


## When to Consider a Discretionary Trust
Discretionary trusts are ideal when the goal is to protect wealth while allowing trustees to adapt to family dynamics. They’re especially useful in these cases:
  * Minor or special needs beneficiaries
  * Blended families with competing interests
  * Heirs who may be financially irresponsible
  * Preserving family businesses or property for future generations

## Common Misunderstandings and the “Disparity with Wills” Problem
Many assume a will alone is sufficient for passing on assets. But a will becomes a public document after death, is subject to probate, and offers little control once finalized. A discretionary trust, in contrast, allows for privacy and proactive distribution management over time. This often leads to what we call the “disparity with wills” effect, where the legal simplicity of a will leads to complicated outcomes for families.
This disparity is especially apparent when unexpected needs arise: a trust can flex, but a will cannot. At Estate Mentors, we often help clients who thought their will covered everything, only to discover that a trust would have avoided disputes or tax surprises.
## Role of the Trustee and How to Choose One
The trustee is the linchpin of a discretionary trust. They’re not just an executor, they’re a long-term decision-maker. That’s why choosing someone with integrity, financial literacy, and emotional maturity is essential.
Some clients select a trusted family member, while others appoint a professional trustee or corporate fiduciary. At Estate Mentors, we help families understand the implications of each option and select a trustee who aligns with their values and intentions.
## Funding and Maintaining the Trust
Creating a discretionary trust is just the beginning. It must be “funded,” meaning that assets, such as bank accounts, real estate, or investments, are formally transferred into the trust. Without funding, the trust offers no protection or control.

## What is the main advantage of a discretionary trust?
It allows trustees to make decisions based on the current needs of beneficiaries, offering flexibility and protection over time.
## Can a discretionary trust be contested?
While any estate plan can be challenged, discretionary trusts offer more privacy and discretion, making them harder to dispute than wills.
