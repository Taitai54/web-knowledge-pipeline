A discretionary trust offers a flexible way to manage and distribute assets. **In a discretionary trust, trustees have the power to decide which beneficiaries receive funds and when**. This flexibility makes them attractive for estate planning, where the exact needs of beneficiaries may change over time.
When setting up a discretionary trust, the settlor, or the person creating the trust, transfers assets to the trustees. The trustees then manage these assets and make decisions about distributions. This can be beneficial for families who want to ensure financial support is available as needs arise without giving immediate, unconditional access to large sums.
Tax implications are an important consideration. While there are benefits, like potential inheritance tax (IHT) savings, there are also certain tax charges to be aware of. Assets transferred into a discretionary trust may face charges if they exceed specific thresholds. Planning carefully with a knowledgeable advisor can help navigate these complexities.
### Key Takeaways
  * Discretionary trusts give trustees control over distributions
  * Setting up involves transferring assets for flexible management
  * Important to consider tax implications


## **What Are Discretionary Trusts?**
Discretionary trusts manage assets based on the decisions of appointed trustees, offering flexibility and tax benefits. These trusts can provide income or capital to various beneficiaries as seen fit.
### Fundamentals of Discretionary Trusts
Discretionary trusts hold assets managed by trustees, who decide how and when to distribute the trust’s income or capital. Unlike fixed trusts, discretionary trusts do not specify exact amounts or times for distribution.
The flexibility of these trusts makes them ideal for adapting to beneficiaries’ circumstances and needs.
By allowing trustees to make these decisions, the trust can address financial or personal changes in the lives of beneficiaries. The trust deed outlines the rules, but trustees have significant freedom.
### Key Roles: Trustees and Beneficiaries
**Trustees** are individuals or organisations managing the trust. They ensure the trust operates according to the settlor’s wishes laid out in the trust deed. Trustees make key decisions, from investment strategies to distributions.
**Beneficiaries** are the individuals or groups who can benefit from the trust. They do not have a fixed right to any part of the trust. Instead, they may receive support depending on the trustees’ discretion. This flexibility helps in tailoring support based on their current needs.
**Settlor** is the person who creates the trust by placing assets into it. The settlor sets the initial terms and appoints the trustees but steps back from direct control once the trust is established.
### Common Types of Discretionary Trusts
There are several types of discretionary trusts, each suited to different needs and situations.
**Family Discretionary Trusts** are common, allowing trustees to support family members in various ways, such as education or health expenses.
**Charitable Discretionary Trusts** focus on distributing funds to charities or specific causes as chosen by the trustees. This type of trust offers flexibility in supporting changing charitable needs.
**Will Trusts** are set up as part of a will, activating upon the settlor’s death. These trusts can provide support to beneficiaries, ensuring that assets are managed and distributed effectively even after the settlor is no longer around.
By understanding these fundamental elements, individuals can better decide if a discretionary trust is suitable for their needs.
## **Setting Up a Discretionary Trust**
Setting up a discretionary trust requires careful planning and consideration. Key areas to focus on include choosing the right trust assets, deciding potential beneficiaries, and drafting a letter of wishes.
### Choosing the Right Trust Assets
Selecting the assets to include in a discretionary trust is crucial. Common assets placed in trusts are property, cash, shares, and valuable items. It’s important to consider the growth potential and tax implications of these assets.
**Property** is a popular choice for many due to its potential for appreciation in value. Cash and **shares** offer liquidity and income through dividends. When choosing assets, consulting a **professional trustee** or getting **legal advice** can ensure the choices align with the trust’s objectives and tax efficiency.
### Deciding Potential Beneficiaries
Beneficiaries can be anyone, but often they are family members like children or grandchildren. The flexibility of a discretionary trust allows trustees to decide how and when to distribute the trust’s assets.
It’s important to list potential beneficiaries explicitly in the trust deed. This can include both primary beneficiaries (e.g., children) and secondary ones (e.g., grandchildren). By clearly defining potential beneficiaries, trustees have a precise understanding of who might benefit from the trust and under what circumstances.
### Drafting a Letter of Wishes
A letter of wishes is an informal document that guides trustees on how to manage and distribute the assets. Although it isn’t legally binding, it carries significant weight in the trustees’ decisions.
The letter should include details like the names of the beneficiaries and the settlor’s preferences on how the assets should be allocated. It might specify preferences for asset distribution, such as prioritising the educational needs of **grandchildren** or providing support for **children** during specific life events. Consulting a solicitor can be beneficial for ensuring the letter is clear and aligns with the trust deed.
## **Tax Considerations and Implications**
When setting up a discretionary trust, it’s important to understand the various tax implications. This includes inheritance tax, capital gains tax, and income tax, as well as possible tax reliefs and exemptions.
### Inheritance Tax and Discretionary Trusts
Inheritance tax (IHT) plays a significant role in discretionary trusts. Assets transferred into a trust above the **nil rate band** (£325,000 as of 2024) are subject to an immediate IHT charge of 20%. Additionally, there is a periodic charge every 10 years, known as the **chargeable lifetime transfer** , which is taxed at up to 6%.
Trusts can reduce the value of an estate subject to IHT, yet it’s essential to be aware of exit charges. When distributing trust assets, beneficiaries might incur a tax known as the **exit charge**. This tax is calculated based on the time assets have been within the trust.
### Capital Gains Tax and Income Tax
Discretionary trusts also face capital gains tax (CGT) and income tax charges. **Capital gains tax** is levied when the trustees dispose of trust assets at a gain. Trustees receive an **annual exemption** , which is usually half the personal allowance (£6,150 for 2024). Gains above this exemption are taxed at 20%, or 28% on residential property.
For income tax, the first £1,000 of income is taxed at 20%. Beyond this threshold, income is taxed at 45%, while dividends are taxed at 39.35%. Income distributed to beneficiaries is taxed at their rate, and trustees must ensure accurate **tax returns** to report these distributions.
### Tax Reliefs and Exemptions
Several tax reliefs and exemptions can affect the taxation of discretionary trusts. For instance, the **annual exemption** allows trustees to benefit from a CGT-free threshold each year. Moreover, specific reliefs, such as **business property relief** and **agricultural property relief** , can reduce the value of certain assets for IHT purposes.
When assets qualify for these reliefs, they can significantly lower the amount of tax owed. It’s crucial for trustees to stay informed about any changes to tax laws and to seek professional advice to utilise all available tax reliefs and exemptions effectively.
## Management and Distribution of Trust Assets
Managing and distributing the assets of a discretionary trust involves the trustees exercising their powers responsibly, making informed decisions about income and capital distribution, and engaging in sound financial planning. This ensures that the trust meets its objectives and protects the interests of the beneficiaries.
### The Trustee’s Powers and Responsibilities
Trustees have substantial power and responsibility in managing the trust. They must adhere strictly to the terms of the trust deed and the letter of wishes, even though the latter is not legally binding. Trustees decide how to invest the trust assets, including selecting  _investment bonds_ and other financial products.
They are also tasked with protecting the trust’s assets from potential risks such as **divorce** , **bankruptcy** , and other poor life choices that could impact beneficiaries’ financial security. Trustees need to balance the needs of current and future beneficiaries, ensuring the trust remains viable and continues to meet its long-term objectives.
### Distributing Income and Capital
Income and capital distribution is at the trustees’ discretion. They decide the amount and timing of distributions based on the beneficiaries’ needs and circumstances. The first £1,000 of trust income is taxed at 20% or 8.75% for dividends. Income over this threshold is subject to higher tax rates, such as 39.35% for dividends.
Trustees must be mindful of tax implications and aim to distribute income and capital efficiently. **Asset protection** is a critical consideration, especially when beneficiaries might face threats like divorce or bankruptcy. Efficient distribution ensures the long-term sustainability of the trust and aligns with its purposes.
### Investment Bonds and Financial Planning
Financial planning is a vital part of managing a discretionary trust. Trustees must make prudent investment decisions to grow the trust’s assets. **Investment bonds** can be an effective tool for achieving this, offering tax advantages and potential for steady income.
Investment strategies should be tailored to the trust’s objectives, balancing risk and return. Proper financial planning also involves regular reviews of the trust’s performance and making adjustments as needed. Trustees may seek professional financial advice to ensure they make well-informed decisions that align with the trust’s goals and beneficiary needs.
